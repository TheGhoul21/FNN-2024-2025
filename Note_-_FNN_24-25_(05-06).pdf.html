Introduction

In this lecture, we will embark on an exploration of linear algebra, focusing on the fundamental concepts of vector spaces and subspaces. These abstract mathematical structures are essential tools in various fields, including computer science, physics, and engineering. We will begin by defining vector spaces and their axioms, then delve into subspaces, linear combinations, span, linear dependence, linear independence, basis, and dimension. Finally, we will touch upon the concept of rank and the Rank-Nullity Theorem. Understanding these concepts is crucial for building a solid foundation in linear algebra and its applications.

Vector Spaces

Definition

Definition 1 (Vector Space). A vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) is a set of objects, called vectors, on which two operations are defined:

  1. Vector Addition: For any two vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}\), there is a unique vector \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}\) called the sum of \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\).

  2. Scalar Multiplication: For any scalar \(c \in \mathbb{R}\) and any vector \(\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}\), there is a unique vector \(c\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}\) called the scalar product of \(c\) and \(\mathbf{u}\).

These operations must satisfy the following axioms.

Axioms

The operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication in a vector space must satisfy a set of axioms. These axioms are divided into those governing vector addition and those governing scalar multiplication.

Vector Addition

For all vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{V}\):

Closure

  1. Closure under Addition: For any \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}\), the sum \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\) is also in \(\mathcal{V}\).

Commutativity

  1. Commutativity of Addition: \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\).

Associativity

  1. Associativity of Addition: \((\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})\).

Zero Vector

  1. Existence of a Zero Vector: There exists a vector \(\mathbf{0} \in \mathcal{V}\), called the zero vector, such that for all \(\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}\), \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{u}\).

Additive Inverses

  1. Existence of Additive Inverses: For each \(\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}\), there exists a vector \(-\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}\), called the additive inverse of \(\mathbf{u}\), such that \(\mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}\).

Scalar Multiplication

For all vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}\) and scalars \(c, d \in \mathbb{R}\):

Closure

  1. Closure under Scalar Multiplication: For any scalar \(c \in \mathbb{R}\) and vector \(\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}\), the scalar product \(c\mathbf{u}\) is also in \(\mathcal{V}\).

Distributivity over Vector Addition

  1. Distributivity of Scalar Multiplication over Vector Addition: \(c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}\).

Distributivity over Scalar Addition

  1. Distributivity of Scalar Multiplication over Scalar Addition: \((c + d)\mathbf{u} = c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u}\).

Scalar Multiplication Identity

  1. Scalar Multiplication Identity: \(1\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}\).

Subspaces

Definition

Definition 2 (Subspace). A subspace \(H\) of a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) is a subset of \(\mathcal{V}\) that is itself a vector space under the same operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication defined on \(\mathcal{V}\).

To verify if a subset is a subspace, we can use the following theorem:

Theorem 1 (Subspace Test). This theorem provides a set of conditions to verify if a subset of a vector space is a subspace. A subset \(H\) of a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) is a subspace of \(\mathcal{V}\) if and only if it satisfies the following three conditions:

  1. Zero Vector: The zero vector of \(\mathcal{V}\) is in \(H\) (\(\mathbf{0} \in H\)).

  2. Closure under Addition: For each \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in H\), the sum \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\) is also in \(H\).

  3. Closure under Scalar Multiplication: For each \(\mathbf{u} \in H\) and scalar \(c \in \mathbb{R}\), the scalar product \(c\mathbf{u}\) is also in \(H\).

Properties

The subspace test provides a practical way to determine if a subset is indeed a subspace by checking for the presence of the zero vector and closure under the two vector space operations.

Zero Vector

A subspace must contain the zero vector of the parent vector space. This ensures that the subspace is not empty and has an additive identity.

Closure under Addition

If we take any two vectors within a subspace and add them, the resulting vector must also remain within the subspace. This ensures that the addition operation is well-defined within the subspace.

Closure under Scalar Multiplication

If we take any vector within a subspace and multiply it by any scalar, the resulting vector must also remain within the subspace. This ensures that the scalar multiplication operation is well-defined within the subspace.

Examples

Trivial Subspaces

Example 1 (Trivial Subspaces). For any vector space \(\mathcal{V}\), there are always at least two subspaces, known as trivial subspaces:

  • The zero subspace \(\{\mathbf{0}\}\), which contains only the zero vector.

  • The vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) itself.

Subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)

Example 2 (Subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) - The x-axis). Let \(\mathcal{V}= \mathbb{R}^2\), and let \(H = \{(x, 0) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}\). \(H\) is the set of all vectors lying on the x-axis in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Let’s check if \(H\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\):

  1. Zero Vector: Is \(\mathbf{0} = (0, 0) \in H\)? Yes, when \(x=0\), we have \((0, 0) \in H\).

  2. Closure under Addition: Let \(\mathbf{u} = (x_1, 0) \in H\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (x_2, 0) \in H\). Then \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (x_1 + x_2, 0)\). Since \(x_1 + x_2\) is a real number, \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in H\).

  3. Closure under Scalar Multiplication: Let \(\mathbf{u} = (x, 0) \in H\) and \(c \in \mathbb{R}\). Then \(c\mathbf{u} = c(x, 0) = (cx, 0)\). Since \(cx\) is a real number, \(c\mathbf{u} \in H\).

Since \(H\) satisfies all three conditions, \(H\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

Non-Examples

Example 3 (Non-Subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)). Let \(\mathcal{V}= \mathbb{R}^2\), and let \(K = \{(x, 1) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}\). \(K\) is the set of all vectors with a y-coordinate of 1. Let’s check if \(K\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\):

  1. Zero Vector: Is \(\mathbf{0} = (0, 0) \in K\)? No, because vectors in \(K\) must have a y-coordinate of 1.

Since \(K\) fails to contain the zero vector, \(K\) is not a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\). (We don’t need to check the other conditions once one condition fails).

Linear Combinations and Span

Linear Combinations

Definition 3 (Linear Combination). Given a set of vectors \(\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}\) in a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\), a linear combination of these vectors is a vector \(\mathbf{y}\) of the form: \(\mathbf{y} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k\) where \(c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k\) are scalars.

Span

Definition 4 (Span). The span of a set of vectors \(S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}\) in a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\), denoted by \(\text{span}(S)\) or \(\text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}\), is the set of all possible linear combinations of \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\). That is, \(\text{span}(S) = \{c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k \mid c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k \in \mathbb{R} \}\)

Span as a Subspace

Theorem 2. This theorem states that the span of any set of vectors within a vector space is always a subspace of that vector space. For any set \(S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}\) in a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\), \(\text{span}(S)\) is a subspace of \(\mathcal{V}\).

Example 4. In \(\mathbb{R}^2\), the span of two non-collinear vectors is \(\mathbb{R}^2\) itself (a plane). The span of two collinear vectors is a line through the origin (a line). The span of the zero vector is just the zero vector itself (the origin).

Linear Dependence and Independence

Linear Dependence

Definition 5 (Linear Dependence). A set of vectors \(\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}\) in a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) is said to be linearly dependent if there exist scalars \(c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k\), not all zero, such that \(c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}\) This is called a non-trivial linear combination that equals the zero vector.

Linear Independence

Definition 6 (Linear Independence). A set of vectors \(\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}\) in a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) is said to be linearly independent if the only scalars \(c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k\) that satisfy \(c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}\) are \(c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_k = 0\). This is called the trivial solution.

Examples in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)

Example 5 (Linear Dependence in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)). Let \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 2)\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (2, 4)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). These vectors are linearly dependent because we can see that \(\mathbf{v}_2 = 2\mathbf{v}_1\), or \(2\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{0}\). Here, \(c_1 = 2\) and \(c_2 = -1\), which are not both zero.

Example 6 (Linear Independence in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)). Let \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (0, 1)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). These vectors are linearly independent. To show this, consider the equation \(c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{0}\), which becomes \(c_1(1, 0) + c_2(0, 1) = (0, 0)\), or \((c_1, c_2) = (0, 0)\). This implies \(c_1 = 0\) and \(c_2 = 0\). Since the only solution is the trivial solution, \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\) are linearly independent.

Basis and Dimension

Definition of a Basis

Definition 7 (Basis). A basis for a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) is a set of vectors \(\mathcal{B} = \{\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n\}\) in \(\mathcal{V}\) such that:

  1. \(\mathcal{B}\) is linearly independent.

  2. \(\mathcal{B}\) spans \(\mathcal{V}\). That is, \(\text{span}(\mathcal{B}) = \mathcal{V}\).

Finite-Dimensional Spaces

Definition 8 (Finite-Dimensional Vector Space). A vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) is said to be finite-dimensional if it has a basis consisting of a finite number of vectors. If a vector space is not finite-dimensional, it is said to be infinite-dimensional.

Definition of Dimension

Definition 9 (Dimension). The dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space \(\mathcal{V}\), denoted by \(\text{dim}(\mathcal{V})\), is the number of vectors in a basis for \(\mathcal{V}\). It is a fundamental property of a vector space, indicating its "size".

Examples

\(\mathbb{R}^2\)

Example 7 (Basis and Dimension of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)). The standard basis for \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is \(\{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2\} = \{(1, 0), (0, 1)\}\). This set is linearly independent and spans \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Therefore, it is a basis for \(\mathbb{R}^2\). The dimension of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is 2, i.e., \(\text{dim}(\mathbb{R}^2) = 2\).

\(\mathbb{R}^3\)

Example 8 (Basis and Dimension of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)). The standard basis for \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is \(\{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \mathbf{e}_3\} = \{(1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0), (0, 0, 1)\}\). This set is linearly independent and spans \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Therefore, it is a basis for \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The dimension of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is 3, i.e., \(\text{dim}(\mathbb{R}^3) = 3\).

\(\mathbb{R}^n\)

Example 9 (Basis and Dimension of \(\mathbb{R}^n\)). Generalizing from \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and \(\mathbb{R}^3\), the standard basis for \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is \(\{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \dots, \mathbf{e}_n\}\), where \(\mathbf{e}_i\) is the vector with a 1 in the \(i\)-th position and 0 elsewhere. This set is linearly independent and spans \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Therefore, it is a basis for \(\mathbb{R}^n\). The dimension of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is \(n\), i.e., \(\text{dim}(\mathbb{R}^n) = n\).

Finding a Basis

Row Reduction

Row reduction is a method used to find a basis for the column space of a matrix, which is the span of its column vectors. Given a set of vectors that span a subspace, we can form a matrix with these vectors as columns and then use row reduction to find a basis.

  1. Form a matrix \(A\) whose columns are the given vectors.

  2. Reduce the matrix \(A\) to row-echelon form using elementary row operations.

  3. Identify the pivot columns in the row-echelon form.

  4. The columns in the original matrix \(A\) that correspond to the pivot columns in the row-echelon form constitute a basis for the column space of \(A\).

Rank

Definition of Rank

Definition 10 (Rank of a Matrix). The rank of a matrix \(A\), denoted by \(\text{rank}(A)\), is the dimension of the column space of \(A\). It is also equal to the dimension of the row space of \(A\). The rank of a matrix represents the number of linearly independent columns (or rows) in the matrix.

Rank-Nullity Theorem

Theorem 3 (Rank-Nullity Theorem). This theorem establishes a relationship between the dimensions of the kernel (null space) and the image (range) of a linear transformation. Let \(\Phi: \mathcal{V}\to \mathcal{W}\) be a linear transformation from a finite-dimensional vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) to a vector space \(\mathcal{W}\). Then, \(\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\Phi)) + \text{dim}(\text{Im}(\Phi)) = \text{dim}(\mathcal{V})\) where \(\text{ker}(\Phi)\) is the kernel (null space) of \(\Phi\) and \(\text{Im}(\Phi)\) is the image (range) of \(\Phi\). In matrix terms, for an \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\), \(\text{dim}(\text{Nul}(A)) + \text{rank}(A) = n\) where \(\text{Nul}(A)\) is the null space of \(A\).

Conclusion

In this lecture, we have laid the groundwork for understanding vector spaces and subspaces. We started with the definition of a vector space and its axioms, then explored subspaces and how to identify them. We introduced linear combinations and span, and discussed the crucial concepts of linear dependence and independence. We defined basis and dimension, and briefly touched upon how to find a basis using row reduction and the definition of rank. Finally, we stated the Rank-Nullity Theorem, which provides a fundamental relationship between the dimensions of the kernel, image, and domain of a linear transformation.

Key takeaways from this lecture include:

  • Vector spaces are fundamental structures in linear algebra defined by a set of axioms.

  • Subspaces are vector spaces within vector spaces, inheriting the same operations.

  • Linear combinations and span help describe how vectors can be combined to form other vectors and spaces.

  • Linear independence and dependence characterize the redundancy or essentiality of vectors in a set.

  • Basis and dimension provide a way to quantify the "size" and structure of a vector space.

  • Rank and the Rank-Nullity Theorem are important tools for understanding linear transformations and matrices.

# Introduction

In this lecture, we will embark on an exploration of linear algebra,
focusing on the fundamental concepts of vector spaces and subspaces.
These abstract mathematical structures are essential tools in various
fields, including computer science, physics, and engineering. We will
begin by defining vector spaces and their axioms, then delve into
subspaces, linear combinations, span, linear dependence, linear
independence, basis, and dimension. Finally, we will touch upon the
concept of rank and the Rank-Nullity Theorem. Understanding these

# Vector Spaces

## Definition

:::: tcolorbox
::: definition
**Definition 1** (Vector Space). A **vector space** $\mathcal{V}$ is a
set of objects, called **vectors**, on which two operations are defined:

1.  **Vector Addition**: For any two vectors
    $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}$, there is a unique vector
    $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}$ called the **sum** of
    $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.

2.  **Scalar Multiplication**: For any scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and any
    vector $\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}$, there is a unique vector
    $c\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}$ called the **scalar product** of $c$
    and $\mathbf{u}$.

These operations must satisfy the following axioms.
:::
::::

## Axioms

The operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication in a vector
space must satisfy a set of axioms. These axioms are divided into those
governing vector addition and those governing scalar multiplication.

### Vector Addition

For all vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{V}$:

#### Closure

1.  **Closure under Addition**: For any
    $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}$, the sum
    $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is also in $\mathcal{V}$.

#### Commutativity

2.  **Commutativity of Addition**:
    $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$.

#### Associativity

3.  **Associativity of Addition**:
    $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$.

#### Zero Vector

4.  **Existence of a Zero Vector**: There exists a vector
    $\mathbf{0} \in \mathcal{V}$, called the **zero vector**, such that
    for all $\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}$,
    $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{u}$.

#### Additive Inverses

5.  **Existence of Additive Inverses**: For each
    $\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}$, there exists a vector
    $-\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}$, called the **additive inverse** of
    $\mathbf{u}$, such that $\mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}$.

### Scalar Multiplication

For all vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}$ and scalars
$c, d \in \mathbb{R}$:

#### Closure

6.  **Closure under Scalar Multiplication**: For any scalar
    $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and vector $\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{V}$, the
    scalar product $c\mathbf{u}$ is also in $\mathcal{V}$.

#### Distributivity over Vector Addition

7.  **Distributivity of Scalar Multiplication over Vector Addition**:
    $c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}$.

#### Distributivity over Scalar Addition

8.  **Distributivity of Scalar Multiplication over Scalar Addition**:
    $(c + d)\mathbf{u} = c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u}$.

#### Scalar Multiplication Identity

9.  **Scalar Multiplication Identity**: $1\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}$.

# Subspaces

## Definition

:::: tcolorbox
::: definition
**Definition 2** (Subspace). A **subspace** $H$ of a vector space
$\mathcal{V}$ is a subset of $\mathcal{V}$ that is itself a vector space
under the same operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication
defined on $\mathcal{V}$.
:::
::::

To verify if a subset is a subspace, we can use the following theorem:

:::: tcolorbox
::: theorem
**Theorem 1** (Subspace Test). *This theorem provides a set of
conditions to verify if a subset of a vector space is a subspace. A
subset $H$ of a vector space $\mathcal{V}$ is a subspace of
$\mathcal{V}$ if and only if it satisfies the following three
conditions:*

1.  ***Zero Vector**: The zero vector of $\mathcal{V}$ is in $H$
    ($\mathbf{0} \in H$).*

2.  ***Closure under Addition**: For each
    $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in H$, the sum $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is
    also in $H$.*

3.  ***Closure under Scalar Multiplication**: For each
    $\mathbf{u} \in H$ and scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$, the scalar product
    $c\mathbf{u}$ is also in $H$.*
:::
::::

## Properties

The subspace test provides a practical way to determine if a subset is
indeed a subspace by checking for the presence of the zero vector and
closure under the two vector space operations.

### Zero Vector

A subspace must contain the zero vector of the parent vector space. This
ensures that the subspace is not empty and has an additive identity.

### Closure under Addition

If we take any two vectors within a subspace and add them, the resulting
vector must also remain within the subspace. This ensures that the
addition operation is well-defined within the subspace.

### Closure under Scalar Multiplication

If we take any vector within a subspace and multiply it by any scalar,
the resulting vector must also remain within the subspace. This ensures
that the scalar multiplication operation is well-defined within the
subspace.

## Examples

### Trivial Subspaces

:::: tcolorbox
::: example
**Example 1** (Trivial Subspaces). For any vector space $\mathcal{V}$,
there are always at least two subspaces, known as **trivial subspaces**:

-   The **zero subspace** $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, which contains only the zero
    vector.

-   The vector space $\mathcal{V}$ itself.
:::
::::

### Subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$

:::: tcolorbox
::: example
**Example 2** (Subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$ - The x-axis). Let
$\mathcal{V}= \mathbb{R}^2$, and let
$H = \{(x, 0) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}$. $H$ is the set
of all vectors lying on the x-axis in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Let's check if $H$
is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$:

1.  **Zero Vector**: Is $\mathbf{0} = (0, 0) \in H$? Yes, when $x=0$, we
    have $(0, 0) \in H$.

2.  **Closure under Addition**: Let $\mathbf{u} = (x_1, 0) \in H$ and
    $\mathbf{v} = (x_2, 0) \in H$. Then
    $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (x_1 + x_2, 0)$. Since $x_1 + x_2$ is a
    real number, $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in H$.

3.  **Closure under Scalar Multiplication**: Let
    $\mathbf{u} = (x, 0) \in H$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$. Then
    $c\mathbf{u} = c(x, 0) = (cx, 0)$. Since $cx$ is a real number,
    $c\mathbf{u} \in H$.

Since $H$ satisfies all three conditions, $H$ is a subspace of
$\mathbb{R}^2$.
:::
::::

## Non-Examples

:::: tcolorbox
::: example
**Example 3** (Non-Subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$). Let
$\mathcal{V}= \mathbb{R}^2$, and let
$K = \{(x, 1) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}$. $K$ is the set
of all vectors with a y-coordinate of 1. Let's check if $K$ is a
subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$:

1.  **Zero Vector**: Is $\mathbf{0} = (0, 0) \in K$? No, because vectors
    in $K$ must have a y-coordinate of 1.

Since $K$ fails to contain the zero vector, $K$ is not a subspace of
$\mathbb{R}^2$. (We don't need to check the other conditions once one
condition fails).
:::
::::

# Linear Combinations and Span

## Linear Combinations

:::: tcolorbox
::: definition
**Definition 3** (Linear Combination). Given a set of vectors
$\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ in a vector space
$\mathcal{V}$, a **linear combination** of these vectors is a vector
$\mathbf{y}$ of the form:
$\mathbf{y} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k$
where $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k$ are scalars.
:::
::::

## Span

:::: tcolorbox
::: definition
**Definition 4** (Span). The **span** of a set of vectors
$S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ in a vector
space $\mathcal{V}$, denoted by $\text{span}(S)$ or
$\text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$, is the
set of all possible linear combinations of
$\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k$. That is,
$\text{span}(S) = \{c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k \mid c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k \in \mathbb{R} \}$
:::
::::

## Span as a Subspace

:::: tcolorbox
::: theorem
**Theorem 2**. *This theorem states that the span of any set of vectors
within a vector space is always a subspace of that vector space. For any
set $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ in a
vector space $\mathcal{V}$, $\text{span}(S)$ is a subspace of
$\mathcal{V}$.*
:::
::::

:::: tcolorbox
::: example
**Example 4**. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, the span of two non-collinear vectors
is $\mathbb{R}^2$ itself (a plane). The span of two collinear vectors is
a line through the origin (a line). The span of the zero vector is just
the zero vector itself (the origin).
:::
::::

# Linear Dependence and Independence

## Linear Dependence

:::: tcolorbox
::: definition
**Definition 5** (Linear Dependence). A set of vectors
$\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ in a vector space
$\mathcal{V}$ is said to be **linearly dependent** if there exist
scalars $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k$, not all zero, such that
$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}$
This is called a **non-trivial linear combination** that equals the zero
vector.
:::
::::

## Linear Independence

:::: tcolorbox
::: definition
**Definition 6** (Linear Independence). A set of vectors
$\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ in a vector space
$\mathcal{V}$ is said to be **linearly independent** if the only scalars
$c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k$ that satisfy
$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}$
are $c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_k = 0$. This is called the **trivial
solution**.
:::
::::

## Examples in $\mathbb{R}^2$

:::: tcolorbox
::: example
**Example 5** (Linear Dependence in $\mathbb{R}^2$). Let
$\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 2)$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (2, 4)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
These vectors are linearly dependent because we can see that
$\mathbf{v}_2 = 2\mathbf{v}_1$, or
$2\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{0}$. Here, $c_1 = 2$ and
$c_2 = -1$, which are not both zero.
:::
::::

:::: tcolorbox
::: example
**Example 6** (Linear Independence in $\mathbb{R}^2$). Let
$\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 0)$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (0, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
These vectors are linearly independent. To show this, consider the
equation $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{0}$, which becomes
$c_1(1, 0) + c_2(0, 1) = (0, 0)$, or $(c_1, c_2) = (0, 0)$. This implies
$c_1 = 0$ and $c_2 = 0$. Since the only solution is the trivial
solution, $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ are linearly independent.
:::
::::

# Basis and Dimension

## Definition of a Basis

:::: tcolorbox
::: definition
**Definition 7** (Basis). A **basis** for a vector space $\mathcal{V}$
is a set of vectors
$\mathcal{B} = \{\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n\}$ in
$\mathcal{V}$ such that:

1.  $\mathcal{B}$ is **linearly independent**.

2.  $\mathcal{B}$ **spans** $\mathcal{V}$. That is,
    $\text{span}(\mathcal{B}) = \mathcal{V}$.
:::
::::

## Finite-Dimensional Spaces

:::: tcolorbox
::: definition
**Definition 8** (Finite-Dimensional Vector Space). A vector space
$\mathcal{V}$ is said to be **finite-dimensional** if it has a basis
consisting of a finite number of vectors. If a vector space is not
finite-dimensional, it is said to be **infinite-dimensional**.
:::
::::

## Definition of Dimension

:::: tcolorbox
::: definition
**Definition 9** (Dimension). The **dimension** of a finite-dimensional
vector space $\mathcal{V}$, denoted by $\text{dim}(\mathcal{V})$, is the
number of vectors in a basis for $\mathcal{V}$. It is a fundamental
property of a vector space, indicating its \"size\".
:::
::::

## Examples

### $\mathbb{R}^2$

:::: tcolorbox
::: example
**Example 7** (Basis and Dimension of $\mathbb{R}^2$). The **standard
basis** for $\mathbb{R}^2$ is
$\{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2\} = \{(1, 0), (0, 1)\}$. This set is
linearly independent and spans $\mathbb{R}^2$. Therefore, it is a basis
for $\mathbb{R}^2$. The dimension of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is 2, i.e.,
$\text{dim}(\mathbb{R}^2) = 2$.
:::
::::

### $\mathbb{R}^3$

:::: tcolorbox
::: example
**Example 8** (Basis and Dimension of $\mathbb{R}^3$). The **standard
basis** for $\mathbb{R}^3$ is
$\{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \mathbf{e}_3\} = \{(1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0), (0, 0, 1)\}$.
This set is linearly independent and spans $\mathbb{R}^3$. Therefore, it
is a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$. The dimension of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is 3,
i.e., $\text{dim}(\mathbb{R}^3) = 3$.
:::
::::

### $\mathbb{R}^n$

:::: tcolorbox
::: example
**Example 9** (Basis and Dimension of $\mathbb{R}^n$). Generalizing from
$\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$, the **standard basis** for
$\mathbb{R}^n$ is $\{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \dots, \mathbf{e}_n\}$,
where $\mathbf{e}_i$ is the vector with a 1 in the $i$-th position and 0
elsewhere. This set is linearly independent and spans $\mathbb{R}^n$.
Therefore, it is a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$. The dimension of
$\mathbb{R}^n$ is $n$, i.e., $\text{dim}(\mathbb{R}^n) = n$.
:::
::::

## Finding a Basis

### Row Reduction

**Row reduction** is a method used to find a basis for the column space
of a matrix, which is the span of its column vectors. Given a set of
vectors that span a subspace, we can form a matrix with these vectors as
columns and then use row reduction to find a basis.

1.  Form a matrix $A$ whose columns are the given vectors.

2.  Reduce the matrix $A$ to row-echelon form using elementary row
    operations.

3.  Identify the pivot columns in the row-echelon form.

4.  The columns in the **original matrix** $A$ that correspond to the
    pivot columns in the row-echelon form constitute a basis for the
    column space of $A$.

# Rank

## Definition of Rank

:::: tcolorbox
::: definition
**Definition 10** (Rank of a Matrix). The **rank** of a matrix $A$,
denoted by $\text{rank}(A)$, is the dimension of the column space of
$A$. It is also equal to the dimension of the row space of $A$. The rank
of a matrix represents the number of linearly independent columns (or
rows) in the matrix.
:::
::::

## Rank-Nullity Theorem

:::: tcolorbox
::: theorem
**Theorem 3** (Rank-Nullity Theorem). *This theorem establishes a
relationship between the dimensions of the kernel (null space) and the
image (range) of a linear transformation. Let
$\Phi: \mathcal{V}\to \mathcal{W}$ be a linear transformation from a
finite-dimensional vector space $\mathcal{V}$ to a vector space
$\mathcal{W}$. Then,
$\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\Phi)) + \text{dim}(\text{Im}(\Phi)) = \text{dim}(\mathcal{V})$
where $\text{ker}(\Phi)$ is the kernel (null space) of $\Phi$ and
$\text{Im}(\Phi)$ is the image (range) of $\Phi$. In matrix terms, for
an $m \times n$ matrix $A$,
$\text{dim}(\text{Nul}(A)) + \text{rank}(A) = n$ where $\text{Nul}(A)$
is the null space of $A$.*
:::
::::
