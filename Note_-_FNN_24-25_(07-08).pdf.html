Introduction

In this lecture, we delve into the concept of general inner products, extending our understanding of vector spaces beyond the familiar Euclidean space. We will explore how inner products generalize the notions of length, angle, and orthogonality, which are fundamental in various areas of mathematics, physics, and engineering, especially in machine learning. Key concepts we will cover include bilinear mappings, symmetric and positive definite conditions, the formal definition of an inner product, and its representation using basis vectors and matrices. Understanding inner products provides a powerful framework for abstracting geometric concepts and solving a wide range of problems in vector spaces.

General Inner Products

Motivation

Generalizing Length and Angle

We begin by recalling the concept of the Euclidean norm and its close relationship with the dot product. As previously discussed, the Euclidean norm is indeed derived from the dot product. In fact, the dot product is a specific instance of a more general structure known as an inner product. Inner products are instrumental in extending the concepts of length and angle from Euclidean space to more abstract vector spaces.

Defining Orthogonality

A primary application of inner products is to define and determine orthogonality between vectors. Orthogonality, or perpendicularity, is a cornerstone concept in linear algebra and geometry. Inner products furnish us with the means to define and verify orthogonality in a broad and versatile manner, applicable to various vector spaces beyond the standard Euclidean setting.

Bilinear Mappings

Definition

To formally define an inner product, we first introduce the concept of a bilinear mapping. The term "bilinear" signifies linearity in two arguments. A bilinear mapping takes two vectors as input and exhibits linearity with respect to each input separately.

Definition 1 (Bilinear Mapping). Let \(\mathcal{V}\) be a vector space over \(\mathbb{R}\). A mapping \(\Omega: \mathcal{V}\times \mathcal{V}\to \mathbb{R}\) is called a bilinear mapping if for all vectors \(x, y, z \in \mathcal{V}\) and scalars \(\lambda, \psi \in \mathbb{R}\), it satisfies the following properties:

  1. Linearity in the first argument: \[\Omega(\lambda x + \psi y, z) = \lambda \Omega(x, z) + \psi \Omega(y, z)\]

  2. Linearity in the second argument: \[\Omega(x, \lambda y + \psi z) = \lambda \Omega(x, y) + \psi \Omega(x, z)\]

In essence, bilinearity means that the mapping distributes linearly over each component when dealing with linear combinations of vectors. We can view bilinear mappings as a specific subset of functions between pairs of vectors that possess this desirable linearity property.

Properties of Bilinearity

Bilinear mappings generalize the concept of linear transformations to two arguments. They are fundamental in defining inner products and other important structures in linear algebra.

Symmetric and Positive Definite Mappings

Among bilinear mappings, we are particularly interested in those that are also symmetric and positive definite. These additional conditions endow them with properties that are crucial for generalizing geometric notions like length and angle.

Symmetry Condition

Definition 2 (Symmetric Bilinear Mapping). A bilinear mapping \(\Omega: \mathcal{V}\times \mathcal{V}\to \mathbb{R}\) is called symmetric if for all \(x, y \in \mathcal{V}\): \[\Omega(x, y) = \Omega(y, x)\]

Symmetry implies that the order of the arguments does not affect the result of the mapping. Swapping the input vectors \(x\) and \(y\) yields the same output value.

Positive Definite Condition

Definition 3 (Positive Definite Bilinear Mapping). A bilinear mapping \(\Omega: \mathcal{V}\times \mathcal{V}\to \mathbb{R}\) is called positive definite if it satisfies two conditions:

  1. For all non-zero vectors \(x \in \mathcal{V}\setminus \{0\}\): \[\Omega(x, x) > 0\]

  2. For the zero vector \(0 \in \mathcal{V}\): \[\Omega(0, 0) = 0\]

This can be compactly written as: \[\forall x \in \mathcal{V}\setminus \{0\} : \Omega(x,x) > 0, \quad \text{and} \quad \Omega(0, 0) = 0\]

Positive definiteness ensures that when the mapping is applied to a vector and itself, the result is always positive, except when the vector is the zero vector, in which case the result is zero. This property is essential for defining notions of length and distance, as we will see later.

Definition of an Inner Product

The properties of bilinearity, symmetry, and positive definiteness are precisely what we need to formally define an inner product.

Inner Product Space

Definition 4 (Inner Product). Let \(\mathcal{V}\) be a vector space over \(\mathbb{R}\). A bilinear mapping \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : \mathcal{V}\times \mathcal{V}\to \mathbb{R}\) is called an inner product on \(\mathcal{V}\) if it is both symmetric and positive definite. We often use the notation \(\langle x, y \rangle\) instead of \(\Omega(x, y)\) to denote the inner product of vectors \(x\) and \(y\).

Definition 5 (Inner Product Space). A pair \((\mathcal{V}, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)\) consisting of a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) and an inner product \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle\) defined on it is called an inner product space or a real vector space with inner product.

Thus, an inner product is essentially a positive definite, symmetric bilinear mapping.

Euclidean Vector Space as a Special Case

Definition 6 (Euclidean Vector Space). If the inner product used on a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) is the dot product (as defined in previous lectures, e.g., equation (3.5) in the textbook), then \((\mathcal{V}, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)\) is called a Euclidean vector space.

The familiar dot product provides a standard example of an inner product, leading to the concept of Euclidean vector spaces. However, it is crucial to recognize that the dot product is not the only possible inner product.

Example: A Non-Standard Inner Product

Example 7 (Inner Product That Is Not the Dot Product). Consider the vector space \(\mathcal{V}= \mathbb{R}^2\). Define a mapping \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : \mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) as follows: For \(x = (x_1, x_2)^T\) and \(y = (y_1, y_2)^T\), \[\langle x, y \rangle := x_1y_1 - (x_1y_2 + x_2y_1) + 2x_2y_2\] This inner product, defined by equation (3.9) in the transcription, is different from the standard dot product on \(\mathbb{R}^2\). It can be verified as an exercise to show that this mapping is indeed an inner product by proving that it satisfies bilinearity, symmetry, and positive definiteness.

This example illustrates that inner products can be defined in ways that are distinct from the conventional dot product, expanding the scope of inner product spaces.

Inner Products and Bases

Representing Vectors Using a Basis

We know that a basis for a vector space provides a way to represent any vector in that space as a linear combination of basis vectors. Let \(\{b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_n\}\) be a basis for a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\). Then, any vectors \(x, y \in \mathcal{V}\) can be represented as: \[\label{eq:vector_representation_x} x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \psi_i b_i\] \[\label{eq:vector_representation_y} y = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \lambda_j b_j\] where \(\psi_i\) and \(\lambda_j\) are scalar coefficients.

Expressing Inner Products with Basis Vectors

Given a basis, we can express the inner product of two vectors in terms of their coordinates with respect to that basis.

Derivation of the Formula

Let \(x\) and \(y\) be represented as in equations [eq:vector_representation_x] and [eq:vector_representation_y]. Using the bilinearity of the inner product, we can expand \(\langle x, y \rangle\) as: \[\begin{aligned} \langle x, y \rangle &= \langle \sum_{i=1}^{n} \psi_i b_i, \sum_{j=1}^{n} \lambda_j b_j \rangle \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \psi_i \lambda_j \langle b_i, b_j \rangle \end{aligned}\] Rearranging the terms, we get: \[\label{eq:inner_product_basis_expansion} \langle x, y \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \psi_i \langle b_i, b_j \rangle \lambda_j\]

Matrix Representation of Inner Products

The Matrix A and its Entries

Define a matrix \(A\) of size \(n \times n\) where the entry \(A_{ij}\) is given by the inner product of the basis vectors \(b_i\) and \(b_j\): \[\label{eq:matrix_A_definition} A_{ij} = \langle b_i, b_j \rangle\] The matrix \(A\) is determined by the choice of basis \(\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}\) and the inner product \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle\).

Inner Product as a Matrix Product: \(\psi^T A \lambda\)

Using the matrix \(A\), we can express the inner product \(\langle x, y \rangle\) in matrix form. Let \(\psi = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 \\ \vdots \\ \psi_n \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 \\ \vdots \\ \lambda_n \end{pmatrix}\) be the column vectors of coefficients of \(x\) and \(y\) respectively. Then, the inner product can be written as a matrix product: \[\label{eq:inner_product_matrix_form} \langle x, y \rangle = \psi^T A \lambda\] where \(\psi^T\) is the transpose of \(\psi\). This formula shows that once we have a basis and the matrix \(A\) (whose entries are inner products of basis vectors), we can compute the inner product of any two vectors \(x\) and \(y\) by knowing their coordinate vectors \(\psi\) and \(\lambda\) with respect to the basis.

Remark. Remark 8. The inner product is fully described by the coordinates with respect to a chosen basis and the matrix \(A\). This matrix \(A\) has special properties due to the properties of the inner product itself (symmetry and positive definiteness). Specifically, \(A\) will be a symmetric and positive definite matrix.

Conclusion

In this lecture, we generalized the concept of the dot product to inner products, which are bilinear, symmetric, and positive definite mappings. We saw that inner products allow us to extend notions of length, angle, and orthogonality to general vector spaces. We defined bilinear mappings (1), symmetry (2), and positive definiteness (3), culminating in the definition of an inner product (4) and inner product space (5). We explored an example of a non-standard inner product (7) and derived the matrix representation of inner products using a basis ([eq:inner_product_matrix_form]). This matrix representation is a powerful tool for computation and analysis.

Further topics to consider include the properties of the matrix \(A\) ([eq:matrix_A_definition]), its symmetry and positive definiteness (as noted in 8), and how these properties relate back to the inner product. We will also explore how inner products are used to define norms, distances, and orthogonality more formally in subsequent lectures.

# Inner Products and Bases

## Representing Vectors Using a Basis

We know that a basis for a vector space provides a way to represent any
vector in that space as a linear combination of basis vectors. Let
$\{b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_n\}$ be a basis for a vector space $\mathcal{V}$.
Then, any vectors $x, y \in \mathcal{V}$ can be represented as:
$$\label{eq:vector_representation_x}
x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \psi_i b_i$$ $$\label{eq:vector_representation_y}
y = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \lambda_j b_j$$ where $\psi_i$ and $\lambda_j$ are
scalar coefficients.

## Expressing Inner Products with Basis Vectors

Given a basis, we can express the inner product of two vectors in terms
of their coordinates with respect to that basis.

### Derivation of the Formula

Let $x$ and $y$ be represented as in equations
[\[eq:vector_representation_x\]](#eq:vector_representation_x){reference-type="eqref"
reference="eq:vector_representation_x"} and
[\[eq:vector_representation_y\]](#eq:vector_representation_y){reference-type="eqref"
reference="eq:vector_representation_y"}. Using the bilinearity of the
inner product, we can expand $\langle x, y \rangle$ as:
$$\begin{aligned}
\langle x, y \rangle &= \langle \sum_{i=1}^{n} \psi_i b_i, \sum_{j=1}^{n} \lambda_j b_j \rangle \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \psi_i \lambda_j \langle b_i, b_j \rangle
\end{aligned}$$ Rearranging the terms, we get:
$$\label{eq:inner_product_basis_expansion}
\langle x, y \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \psi_i  \langle b_i, b_j \rangle \lambda_j$$

## Matrix Representation of Inner Products

### The Matrix A and its Entries

Define a matrix $A$ of size $n \times n$ where the entry $A_{ij}$ is
given by the inner product of the basis vectors $b_i$ and $b_j$:
$$\label{eq:matrix_A_definition}
A_{ij} = \langle b_i, b_j \rangle$$ The matrix $A$ is determined by the
choice of basis $\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}$ and the inner product
$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$.

### Inner Product as a Matrix Product: $\psi^T A \lambda$

Using the matrix $A$, we can express the inner product
$\langle x, y \rangle$ in matrix form. Let
$\psi = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 \\ \vdots \\ \psi_n \end{pmatrix}$ and
$\lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 \\ \vdots \\ \lambda_n \end{pmatrix}$
be the column vectors of coefficients of $x$ and $y$ respectively. Then,
the inner product can be written as a matrix product:
$$\label{eq:inner_product_matrix_form}
\langle x, y \rangle = \psi^T A \lambda$$ where $\psi^T$ is the
transpose of $\psi$. This formula shows that once we have a basis and
the matrix $A$ (whose entries are inner products of basis vectors), we
can compute the inner product of any two vectors $x$ and $y$ by knowing
their coordinate vectors $\psi$ and $\lambda$ with respect to the basis.

**Remark 8**. The inner product is fully described by the coordinates
