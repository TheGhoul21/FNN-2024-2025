Introduction

This lecture introduces the concept of general inner products, extending the familiar notion of the dot product in Euclidean space. Inner products serve as a powerful tool to generalize the concepts of length, angle, and orthogonality in vector spaces. We will define inner products formally by examining:

  • Bilinear mappings

  • Symmetry

  • Positive definiteness

Furthermore, we will demonstrate how inner products can be represented using basis vectors and matrices. Understanding inner products provides a robust framework for abstracting geometric concepts and is crucial in various fields, including linear algebra, geometry, and machine learning.

General Inner Products

Motivation

Generalizing Length and Angle

The Euclidean norm, closely related to the dot product, motivates the concept of general inner products. The dot product is a specific type of inner product, which allows us to generalize the notions of length and angle from Euclidean space to abstract vector spaces.

Defining Orthogonality

A key application of inner products is to define orthogonality. Orthogonality, or perpendicularity, is fundamental in linear algebra and geometry. Inner products provide a generalized way to define and determine orthogonality in various vector spaces.

Bilinear Mappings

Definition

Inner products are defined based on bilinear mappings. A bilinear mapping takes two vectors as input and is linear in each argument separately.

Definition 1 (Bilinear Mapping). Let \(\mathcal{V}\) be a vector space over \(\mathbb{R}\). A mapping \(\Omega: \mathcal{V}\times \mathcal{V}\to \mathbb{R}\) is a bilinear mapping if for all \(x, y, z \in \mathcal{V}\) and scalars \(\lambda, \psi \in \mathbb{R}\):

  1. \(\Omega(\lambda x + \psi y, z) = \lambda \Omega(x, z) + \psi \Omega(y, z)\) (Linearity in the first argument)

  2. \(\Omega(x, \lambda y + \psi z) = \lambda \Omega(x, y) + \psi \Omega(x, z)\) (Linearity in the second argument)

Bilinear mappings are linear in each argument, which is a crucial property for defining inner products.

Symmetric and Positive Definite Mappings

For an inner product, a bilinear mapping must also be symmetric and positive definite. These properties are essential for generalizing geometric concepts.

Symmetry Condition

Definition 2 (Symmetric Bilinear Mapping). A bilinear mapping \(\Omega: \mathcal{V}\times \mathcal{V}\to \mathbb{R}\) is symmetric if for all \(x, y \in \mathcal{V}\): \[\Omega(x, y) = \Omega(y, x)\]

Symmetry means the order of vectors in the mapping does not affect the result.

Positive Definite Condition

Definition 3 (Positive Definite Bilinear Mapping). A bilinear mapping \(\Omega: \mathcal{V}\times \mathcal{V}\to \mathbb{R}\) is positive definite if:

  1. \(\forall x \in \mathcal{V}\setminus \{0\} : \Omega(x, x) > 0\)

  2. \(\Omega(0, 0) = 0\)

Positive definiteness ensures that \(\Omega(x,x)\) is strictly positive for any non-zero vector \(x\), which is vital for defining length and distance.

Definition of an Inner Product

An inner product is defined as a bilinear mapping that is both symmetric and positive definite.

Inner Product Space

Definition 4 (Inner Product). Let \(\mathcal{V}\) be a vector space over \(\mathbb{R}\). An inner product on \(\mathcal{V}\) is a mapping \((\cdot, \cdot): \mathcal{V}\times \mathcal{V}\to \mathbb{R}\) that is bilinear, symmetric, and positive definite. We denote the inner product of vectors \(x\) and \(y\) as \((x, y)\).

Definition 5 (Inner Product Space). An inner product space is a pair \((\mathcal{V}, (\cdot, \cdot))\) consisting of a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\) and an inner product \((\cdot, \cdot)\) defined on \(\mathcal{V}\).

Euclidean Vector Space

Definition 6 (Euclidean Vector Space). A Euclidean vector space is an inner product space where the inner product is the dot product.

The dot product is a specific example of an inner product, leading to Euclidean vector spaces. However, other inner products exist.

Example: A Non-Standard Inner Product

Example 7 (Inner Product That Is Not the Dot Product). Consider \(\mathcal{V}= \mathbb{R}^2\). Define \((x, y) := x_1y_1 - (x_1y_2 + x_2y_1) + 2x_2y_2\) for \(x = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(y = \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \end{pmatrix}\). This defines an inner product on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) different from the dot product. Verifying that this mapping is indeed an inner product (i.e., bilinear, symmetric, and positive definite) is left as an exercise.

This example demonstrates that inner products are not unique and can be defined differently from the standard dot product.

Inner Products and Bases

Representing Vectors Using a Basis

Let \(\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}\) be a basis for a vector space \(\mathcal{V}\). Any vectors \(x, y \in \mathcal{V}\) can be represented as linear combinations of these basis vectors: \[\label{eq:vector_representation_x} x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \psi_i b_i\] \[\label{eq:vector_representation_y} y = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \lambda_j b_j\] where \(\psi_i\) and \(\lambda_j\) are scalar coefficients, representing the coordinates of \(x\) and \(y\) with respect to the basis \(\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}\).

Expressing Inner Products via Basis Vectors

Given a basis, we can express the inner product of any two vectors \(x\) and \(y\) in terms of their basis representations.

Derivation

Using the bilinear property of inner products, we can expand \((x, y)\) as follows: \[\begin{aligned} (x, y) &= \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \psi_i b_i, \sum_{j=1}^{n} \lambda_j b_j \right) \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \psi_i \lambda_j (b_i, b_j) \end{aligned}\] Thus, the inner product \((x, y)\) can be expressed as a double summation involving the coefficients \(\psi_i, \lambda_j\) and the inner products of the basis vectors \((b_i, b_j)\). Rearranging the terms, we have: \[\label{eq:inner_product_basis_expansion} (x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \psi_i (b_i, b_j) \lambda_j\]

Matrix Representation

The expression in [eq:inner_product_basis_expansion] can be represented in matrix form, which provides a convenient way to compute inner products.

Defining the Matrix A

Let \(A\) be an \(n \times n\) matrix where the \((i, j)\)-th entry \(A_{ij}\) is the inner product of the \(i\)-th and \(j\)-th basis vectors: \[\label{eq:matrix_A_definition} A_{ij} = (b_i, b_j)\] The matrix \(A\) is determined by the chosen basis \(\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}\) and the inner product \((\cdot, \cdot)\).

Inner Product as Matrix Multiplication

Let \(\psi = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 \\ \vdots \\ \psi_n \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 \\ \vdots \\ \lambda_n \end{pmatrix}\) be the coordinate vectors of \(x\) and \(y\) with respect to the basis \(\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}\). Then, the inner product \((x, y)\) can be written in matrix notation as: \[\label{eq:inner_product_matrix_form} (x, y) = \psi^T A \lambda\] where \(\psi^T\) is the transpose of the column vector \(\psi\). This matrix representation shows that the inner product of any two vectors can be computed using their coordinate vectors and the matrix \(A\), which is constructed from the inner products of the basis vectors.

Remark. Remark 8. The inner product is completely determined by the choice of basis and the matrix \(A\), where \(A_{ij} = (b_i, b_j)\). Due to the properties of the inner product (symmetry and positive definiteness), the matrix \(A\) is guaranteed to be symmetric and positive definite.

Conclusion

In this lecture, we generalized the concept of the dot product to the broader notion of inner products. We established that inner products are bilinear, symmetric, and positive definite mappings, which extend the geometric concepts of length, angle, and orthogonality to general vector spaces. Key definitions introduced include:

  • Bilinear Mapping (1): A mapping linear in each argument.

  • Symmetric Bilinear Mapping (2): A bilinear mapping where the order of vectors does not matter.

  • Positive Definite Bilinear Mapping (3): A bilinear mapping that is strictly positive for non-zero vectors when applied to a vector and itself.

  • Inner Product (4): A bilinear mapping that is both symmetric and positive definite.

  • Inner Product Space (5): A vector space equipped with an inner product.

We examined an example of a non-standard inner product on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) (7) and derived the matrix representation of inner products using a basis ([eq:inner_product_matrix_form]). This representation shows that an inner product can be fully described by a matrix \(A\), where \(A_{ij} = (b_i, b_j)\) for a chosen basis \(\{b_i\}\). The matrix \(A\) is symmetric and positive definite (8).

In subsequent lectures, we will explore the properties of the matrix \(A\) in more detail and investigate how inner products are used to formally define norms, distances, and orthogonality in vector spaces, building upon the foundations laid in this lecture.

# Inner Products and Bases

## Representing Vectors Using a Basis

Let $\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}$ be a basis for a vector space $\mathcal{V}$.
Any vectors $x, y \in \mathcal{V}$ can be represented as linear
combinations of these basis vectors:
$$\label{eq:vector_representation_x}
x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \psi_i b_i$$ $$\label{eq:vector_representation_y}
y = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \lambda_j b_j$$ where $\psi_i$ and $\lambda_j$ are
scalar coefficients, representing the coordinates of $x$ and $y$ with
respect to the basis $\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}$.

## Expressing Inner Products via Basis Vectors

Given a basis, we can express the inner product of any two vectors $x$
and $y$ in terms of their basis representations.

### Derivation

Using the bilinear property of inner products, we can expand $(x, y)$ as
follows: $$\begin{aligned}
(x, y) &= \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \psi_i b_i, \sum_{j=1}^{n} \lambda_j b_j \right) \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \psi_i \lambda_j (b_i, b_j)
\end{aligned}$$ Thus, the inner product $(x, y)$ can be expressed as a
double summation involving the coefficients $\psi_i, \lambda_j$ and the
inner products of the basis vectors $(b_i, b_j)$. Rearranging the terms,
we have: $$\label{eq:inner_product_basis_expansion}
(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \psi_i (b_i, b_j) \lambda_j$$

The expression in
[\[eq:inner_product_basis_expansion\]](#eq:inner_product_basis_expansion){reference-type="eqref"
reference="eq:inner_product_basis_expansion"} can be represented in
matrix form, which provides a convenient way to compute inner products.

### Defining the Matrix A

Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix where the $(i, j)$-th entry $A_{ij}$
is the inner product of the $i$-th and $j$-th basis vectors:
$$\label{eq:matrix_A_definition}
A_{ij} = (b_i, b_j)$$ The matrix $A$ is determined by the chosen basis
$\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}$ and the inner product $(\cdot, \cdot)$.

### Inner Product as Matrix Multiplication

Let $\psi = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 \\ \vdots \\ \psi_n \end{pmatrix}$
and
$\lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 \\ \vdots \\ \lambda_n \end{pmatrix}$
be the coordinate vectors of $x$ and $y$ with respect to the basis
$\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}$. Then, the inner product $(x, y)$ can be written
in matrix notation as: $$\label{eq:inner_product_matrix_form}
(x, y) = \psi^T A \lambda$$ where $\psi^T$ is the transpose of the
column vector $\psi$. This matrix representation shows that the inner
product of any two vectors can be computed using their coordinate
vectors and the matrix $A$, which is constructed from the inner products
of the basis vectors.
